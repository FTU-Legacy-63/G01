## **5\. How my work is used in the final product**

My work contributes to both the content and the underlying structure of Pitch & Ledger.

At the case level, the founder pitch introduces the opportunity, the Q\&A guides the investigation, and the evidence architecture connects management's claims with financial, operating, and industry information. The checklist then converts the player's interpretation into structured assessments, while case-level learning feedback explains the strengths and risks identified or overlooked.

At the project level, the reusable case-design framework provides a common structure for multiple startups. Case 1 focuses on the difference between commercial traction and financial sustainability, whereas Case 2 focuses on marketplace quality, monetization, and valuation. The same general methodology can therefore support different investment lessons without making every case follow the same conclusion.

The framework also provides a reference for the development of Case 3 to Kiều Anh (Case 3’s writer). This helps the team extend the game while maintaining consistency in case structure, evidence-based reasoning, checklist design, and feedback.

My wider review and cross-checking contributions support the integration of these case materials with the team's financial analysis, gameplay rules, and user flow.

**7\. Remaining limitation**

The framework improves consistency and reusability, but the cases still rely on predefined scenarios, benchmark assessments, and simulated investment outcomes. They cannot fully reproduce the uncertainty, negotiation, and changing assumptions of real venture-capital decisions. The quality of the learning experience also depends on the accuracy and consistency of the financial assumptions and evidence.

In addition, the case narratives and character dialogue have not yet been evaluated through structured user testing or formally grounded in a validated learning-design framework. The next step is to test the cases with actual learners, observe where they struggle, and revise the narrative, dialogue, checklist, and feedback based on evidence.

## **8\. Message to future students**

**Start with the problem, then find the solution \- not the other way around.**  
Before deciding what to build, identify a real problem, understand who experiences it, and determine why it matters. Do not start with an attractive idea and then look for a problem to justify it. Keep returning to the original problem and ask whether each feature and design decision helps solve it. The goal is not simply to build something impressive, but to create something that delivers value to its intended users.

**Build something others can reuse, and keep evidence of how it was used.**  
Do not document only the final files you produce. Preserve the framework behind them, the reasoning behind important decisions, the revisions made after feedback, and the way your work supports other parts of the product. A reusable foundation can have a greater impact when other team members build on it. The first version does not need to be perfect; applying it, receiving feedback, and refining it are part of the process.

Keep evidence of that process. If your framework helps another team member develop a later component, preserve the original framework, the resulting work, and records showing the connection. Version histories, comments, and review notes can demonstrate individual impact more clearly than final files alone. Meaningful progress comes from building, testing, learning, and refining.
